Local Knowledge

Dayton Doesn't Have One Kind of House

Between the Oregon District and a 1960s Belmont ranch there are eighty years and three wall materials. A company that washes both the same way is going to ruin one of them.

The historic neighborhoods: South Park, St. Anne's Hill, Grafton Hill, the Oregon District

Victorian and Italianate homes with painted wood clapboard, decorative trim, and in many cases a paint history that goes back before 1978 — which means possible lead-based layers underneath. High pressure on that wall does two things at once: it destroys the paint film, and it aerosolizes whatever is in it. We soft wash these homes at minimal pressure with controlled rinse, and if the existing paint is already chalking or peeling we'll tell you straight that the house needs paint more than it needs washing.

The post-war ring: Belmont, Riverside, Harrison Township, Northridge

Ranches and Cape Cods with vinyl and aluminum siding. Aluminum from this era chalks; vinyl gets a green algae film on the shaded elevations and, near the older industrial corridors, a gray haze that isn't biological at all. Different problems, different detergent, one visit.

Brick and mixed-material homes

Plenty of Dayton brick has painted or stained trim, wood soffit and fascia, and vinyl on the gables. That mix is exactly where careless washing shows up later — a pressure setting fine for the brick will chew the soffit. We change technique as we move around the house rather than picking one setting for the whole job.

Why the north wall is always the worst

The Great Miami and Mad River valleys keep humidity up all summer, and the north face of a house gets the least direct sun, so it dries slowest. Algae takes what it can get: shade plus moisture. That's why the north wall is green while the south wall looks fine, and why the black streaks on your roof start on the north slope. Nothing is wrong with your house — it's just the shady side.
