Deck Cleaning Before Staining

Three prep failures account for nearly every peeling, blotchy deck stain — and all three happen before the can is opened.

By Anthony Leonard, A Team Contracting · August 7, 2026 · Tipp City, Ohio

On this page

  1. Why prep decides the outcome
  2. The three failures
  3. The order that works
  4. Composite decking is different
  5. We do the washing part

Nearly every peeling, blotchy deck stain traces back to one of three prep failures — and all three happen before the can is opened. If you're staining this season, this is the part that decides whether it lasts three years or one.

Why prep decides the outcome

Stain has to penetrate. That's the whole mechanism — unlike paint, which forms a film on top, stain works by soaking into the wood fibers. Anything sitting between the stain and those fibers stops it: mildew, the gray oxidized surface layer, old failing finish, mill glaze on new boards, or water already occupying the pores.

A deck that looks clean at standing height is usually not clean at the fiber level. That gap is why so many stain jobs fail in the second season.

The three failures

1. Too much pressure

This is the big one. Softwoods — pressure-treated pine, cedar, redwood — have alternating soft and hard grain. Push past roughly 1,200 PSI and you blast the soft grain out from between the hard rings. The result is a furred, fuzzy surface that drinks stain unevenly in some spots and repels it in others, and that never feels smooth underfoot again. There's no undoing it short of sanding the whole deck.

Decks want low pressure, a wide fan tip, consistent distance and a dedicated wood cleaner doing the actual work. Same principle as soft washing siding: chemistry over force.

2. Staining too soon

Wood that's dry to the touch can be holding significant moisture two millimeters down. Stain that goes over it seals that water in, and when the sun heats the board the trapped moisture pushes the finish back off — peeling, blistering and cloudiness within a season.

Minimum 48 hours of dry weather after washing. Longer for shaded decks, dense hardwoods, or anything washed heavily. The reliable answer is a moisture meter: under about 15% and you're clear. Without one, the water test works — sprinkle a little on the boards, and if it soaks in within a minute or two rather than beading, the wood is ready to accept stain.

3. Skipping the brightener

Most deck cleaners are alkaline or hypochlorite-based, and they leave the wood's surface pH high and the color washed-out and dull. A brightener — typically oxalic acid — neutralizes that, restores natural color, and opens the fibers back up. It's a fifteen-minute step that visibly changes the result, and it's the one homeowners skip most often.

The order that works

  1. Clear and inspect. Furniture and planters off. Check for popped fasteners, soft boards and rot at the ledger and post bases — replace before finishing, not after.
  2. Pre-wet the surroundings. Plants and shrubs around the deck get wetted and covered before any cleaner goes down.
  3. Apply wood cleaner. Let it dwell. Don't let it dry on the boards.
  4. Rinse at low pressure. Wide fan, consistent distance, always with the grain, board by board — never stopping mid-board.
  5. Brighten and neutralize. Apply, dwell, rinse thoroughly.
  6. Dry 48+ hours. Check with a meter or the water test. Don't rush this because a free weekend is coming.
  7. Light sanding if needed. Knock back any raised grain, then sweep and blow off the dust.
  8. Stain. Above 50°F, out of direct hot sun, and not with rain forecast in the next 24 hours.

Composite decking is different

Composite doesn't get stained, but it does get cleaned — and it's less forgiving than people expect. High pressure can gouge the cap layer and void some manufacturer warranties outright. Composite gets a soft wash with a cleaner rated for it, full stop. Check your manufacturer's guidance before anyone points a machine at it.

New decks: wait

Fresh pressure-treated lumber often shows up from the yard still wet with treatment chemicals and needs weeks — sometimes months — to dry enough to take stain. Use the water test rather than a calendar. Staining a new deck too early is the most expensive version of this mistake, because you'll be stripping it before you can do it right.
