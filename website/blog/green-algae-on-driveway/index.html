Green Algae on a Driveway

It's slicker than it looks, it always comes back in the same spot, and the reason why tells you exactly how to stop it.

The green-black film creeping across the shaded edge of your driveway is algae, it's slicker than it looks, and getting rid of it permanently has less to do with scrubbing than with figuring out why that particular strip of concrete never dries out.

Why it's growing there and nowhere else

Algae needs three things: moisture, shade and a surface porous enough to hold onto. Concrete is porous, Ohio supplies the moisture, and your trees supply the shade. That's the whole story.

It's why the growth is almost never uniform. The north and east edges of a driveway green up first because they get the least direct sun. The strip under the maple goes before the open middle. The apron next to the downspout goes before anything. If you look at where your algae is, you're looking at a map of where your driveway stays wet.

This matters because it tells you what will actually keep it from coming back — and it isn't more scrubbing.

It's a slip hazard, not just an eyesore

Wet algae on concrete is genuinely treacherous. On a sloped driveway or a set of front steps it's close to walking on ice, and it's at its worst in exactly the conditions where you're least expecting it: a light rain on a warm day. If anyone in the household is elderly or unsteady, this moves from a curb-appeal problem to a safety one.

What works and what doesn't at home

A garden hose: no

Rinsing wets the algae and moves the loose surface layer around. The colony is in the pores. It'll look darker for an hour and identical by tomorrow.

Diluted bleach: partially

Sodium hypochlorite kills algae, and it's the active ingredient in professional treatments too. But killing it and removing it are different jobs. Dead algae residue sits in the surface pores and leaves a grey, blotchy slab that looks washed-out rather than clean. Bleach also runs off — onto your grass, into your beds, and down the storm drain — so concentration and containment matter more than people assume.

A rented pressure washer with a wand: not the way you want

This is where most DIY driveway jobs go wrong. A wand tip cleans a narrow stripe at a time, and unless your overlap is perfect you get the zebra-striped driveway you've seen in your neighborhood — permanent-looking for months. Too much pressure on older concrete also etches the surface, opening the pores further so the algae comes back faster next time. If you rent, rent a surface cleaner attachment, not just a wand.

How it's done properly

  1. Pre-treat. A detergent that kills the organism goes down first and is given time to dwell. Chemistry does the work; the machine only rinses.
  2. Surface clean. A rotary surface cleaner — a spinning bar under a shroud — cleans edge to edge at a consistent distance and pressure. That's what produces an even finish with no wand marks.
  3. Detail the edges. The perimeter, expansion joints and anywhere the surface cleaner can't reach get done by hand.
  4. Rinse everything. Including the beds and the grass along the slab.

Oil stains and rust need their own pre-treatment before any of this, and old oil may only lift 80–95%. Anyone who promises 100% on a decade-old oil stain is guessing.

Making it stay gone

  • Trim what's shading it. More sun on the slab is the single most effective change you can make.
  • Fix the drainage. Redirect a downspout that's dumping onto the concrete. Clear the gutters so overflow isn't sheeting down onto the apron.
  • Sweep the leaf litter. Wet leaves left on concrete hold moisture and stain it with tannin — a separate brown stain that also needs treating.
  • Consider a penetrating sealer once the slab is clean and completely dry. Less absorbed water means a less hospitable surface.
  • Get on an annual cycle. A yearly maintenance wash is quick and cheap. A ten-year rescue job is neither.
