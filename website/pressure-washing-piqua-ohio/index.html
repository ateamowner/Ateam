Local Knowledge

What the Great Miami Does to Piqua Exteriors

Piqua grew up on the river and the canal, and the river is still setting the terms — humidity, shade and a mature tree canopy that keeps a lot of this town damp well into the afternoon.

Shade plus moisture is the entire recipe for algae. Homes on the older, tree-lined streets near the Great Miami and out toward Echo Hills green up noticeably faster than the same house would on an open lot in a new subdivision. It isn't a maintenance failure; it's a microclimate. It does mean an annual wash makes more sense here than the eighteen-month cycle that works fine elsewhere.

Low-lying slabs near the river also pick up a fine silt film after high water. That's cosmetic and comes off easily — it just needs a detergent that can suspend the fines instead of grinding them into the concrete.

Downtown and the older housing stock

Historic brick around Fort Piqua Plaza and the High Street blocks, plus a lot of pre-war housing with original wood and many layers of paint. Everything in that category gets a soft wash, not a wand. Old mortar and old paint both fail under pressure, and neither one goes back on.

The mid-century ranch belt

1950s–70s ranches with original aluminum siding, north of downtown and out toward Covington Avenue. That aluminum has chalked. Pressure washing it streaks it permanently, so it gets detergent, dwell time and a gentle rinse instead.

Hard water is a Miami County constant

The Great Miami buried valley aquifer runs through limestone, so the water coming out of your spigot is loaded with dissolved calcium. Rinse windows with it and you get the white spotting people mistake for streaks. We finish exterior glass with purified water for exactly this reason — it's the difference between clean glass and clean-looking-until-it-dries glass.
